Research

Ancient organelles, underappreciated machines

Cilia are slender, hair-like projections that extend from the surface of many eukaryotic cells. Motile cilia drive the locomotion of unicellular eukaryotes and sperm, clear mucus from the airways, circulate cerebrospinal fluid, and set up the left–right body axis in the embryo.

Primary cilia act as antennae for extracellular signals and play important roles in sensory perception and in the development of many organs and tissues. Our work aims to determine how cilia are formed, how ciliary signaling pathways are established and maintained, and how motile cilia beat.

Structure and function of the axoneme

The axoneme is one of the most geometrically complex macromolecular machines in nature. We use cryo-EM to understand, in atomic detail, how it generates ciliary motility.

Read more

Motile axonemes have a “9+2” architecture: nine doublet microtubules (DMTs), each bound by dynein motors and regulatory complexes, encircle a central pair. Dyneins on one DMT pull against a neighbor to cause sliding and bending, with activity alternating across the axoneme to produce the waveform.

By biochemically deconstructing Chlamydomonas axonemes, we have resolved DMTs and their microtubule inner proteins, outer dynein arms, radial spokes and the central pair, often with Rui Zhang and Susan Dutcher (WashU). In 2023 we consolidated these into a near-complete atomic model of the axoneme repeat.

We are now studying mammalian and human axonemes from airway epithelia and other tissues to understand species-specific adaptations and the causes of motile ciliopathies.

Molecular transport in the cilium

Intraflagellar transport (IFT) moves proteins along ciliary microtubules using train-like assemblies of IFT-A, IFT-B and the BBSome. We want to know how it works and how it maintains signaling.

Read more

IFT-B is implicated in trafficking soluble proteins and axonemal precursors, whereas the BBSome and IFT-A are thought to transport membrane proteins. We are interested in the interplay between IFT complexes, microtubules, motors, adaptors and cargoes.

We determined structures of the native BBSome from bovine retina, revealing its GTPase-dependent activation by ARL6, a structural relationship to clathrin adaptors, and the basis of Bardet–Biedl syndrome mutations. Our structure of native IFT-A revealed how it polymerizes into anterograde trains.

Methods for interpreting cryo-EM maps

We develop methods to accelerate cryo-EM structure determination and to build all-atom models that are as accurate as possible.

Read more

With Kiarash Jamali and Sjors Scheres at the MRC-LMB, we helped test and extend ModelAngelo, a deep-learning approach to automated model building and de novo identification of proteins in cryo-EM maps. The software is available on GitHub.

Haixia Zhou

Postdoctoral Fellow

Studies how cilia are built, from IFT-A to the ciliary transition zone.

Bio

Haixia received her Ph.D. from Tsinghua University in 2019, where she studied SARS and MERS coronaviruses. She joined the lab in September 2019, initially as a joint postdoc with Dr. Johannes Walter to determine the structure of CRL2Lrr1. Using single-particle cryo-EM, she determined the structure and polymerization mechanism of the IFT-A complex, then used cryo-FIB-ET on human airway cells to determine the in-cell structure of the human ciliary transition zone. She was a BCMP-Merck Postdoctoral Fellow in 2021 and a BCMP Postdoctoral Fellow in 2024.
